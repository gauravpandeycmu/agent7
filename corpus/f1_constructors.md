# Formula One constructors and team structures

A **constructor** in Formula One is the entity that owns the intellectual property of the chassis. Ferrari, McLaren, Williams, and Mercedes (as a works team) are historic constructors. Racing Point / Aston Martin and AlphaTauri / Racing Bulls illustrate rebrands that keep constructor identity for championship points.

The **constructors' championship** awards points from both cars. A team can win constructors while neither driver wins the drivers' title (Mercedes 2021 came close the other way; 2016 was a dual Mercedes lock). Budget cap rules from 2021 limit listed expenditure so wealthy teams cannot infinitely buy development.

**Works** versus **customer** engines: Mercedes, Ferrari, Renault/Alpine, and Honda/Red Bull Powertrains supply power units. Customer teams (Williams historically with Renault then Mercedes; Haas with Ferrari) buy engines and sometimes gearboxes. Aerodynamic independence is required: you may not copy another team's CAD, though the 2020 Racing Point "pink Mercedes" controversy tested the letter of the sporting regulations on listed parts.

**Wind tunnel** and **CFD** allocations are inverse to championship position: slower teams get more aerodynamic testing hours. This is a sporting equalizer, not a physics one.

Factory organization typically splits:
- Vehicle science / aero
- Vehicle performance (tyre models, ride, setup)
- Power unit liaison
- Race engineering (two car groups)
- Strategy
- Operations and pit crew

**Cost cap** loopholes (capitalized infrastructure, driver salaries outside the cap in early years) have been political flashpoints. Red Bull's 2021 breach and the resulting penalty (fine plus aero-test reduction) showed the FIA will use sporting as well as financial sanctions.

**Listed parts** versus **non-listed parts** determine what can be outsourced. Crash structures, survival cell, and aerodynamic surfaces are tightly controlled. Some teams buy gearboxes or suspension from a larger manufacturer under approved arrangements.

Driver market: a constructor signs two race drivers plus reserves. Super licence points gate F2 graduates. Pay drivers still appear at the back of the grid when budget cap and sponsor reality collide.

## Staff, simulators, and the race weekend

A modern works team employs hundreds of people. The **driver-in-loop simulator** at the factory runs the same control software as the car so engineers can test setup changes overnight. Correlation between sim and track is a competitive secret.

**Race weekend roles** include: two race engineers (one per car), performance engineers, a strategist watching GPS and pit windows, a tyre technician reading infrared blankets and pyrometer readings, and a pit crew trained to sub-two-second stops.

**Board-level** decisions (engine freeze, homologation, protest strategy) sit with the team principal and sporting director. Technical directives from the FIA can outlaw a concept mid-season (flexible rear wings, porpoising-related floor stiffness, ride-height maps).

**Academy drivers** in F2/F3 feed the grid. Red Bull's programme produced Verstappen, Gasly, Albon, and others; Ferrari Driver Academy and Mercedes Junior Team do the same. A constructor without an academy often buys talent on the open market.

**Sponsorship** on the livery (title sponsor, technical partners, national oil companies) funds the gap between cost cap and commercial reality. Title-sponsor changes explain many rebrands (Lotus/Renault/Alpine; Sauber/Alfa Romeo/Audi forthcoming works project).

When a query asks which factory builds the chassis, the answer is the constructor of record, not the engine supplier. Honda-powered Red Bull still scores as Red Bull Racing for constructors' points.
