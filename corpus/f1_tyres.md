# Formula One tyres and compounds

Pirelli has been Formula One's sole tyre supplier since 2011. Each Grand Prix weekend typically offers a subset of slick compounds labelled in recent seasons as C1 (hardest) through C5 (softest), plus intermediates and full wets.

Dry slicks trade peak grip against degradation. A softer compound reaches temperature faster and produces more mechanical grip for qualifying and short stints. A harder compound lasts longer and is the usual choice for a one-stop if thermal degradation on the soft would force an extra pit visit.

**Thermal degradation** is the loss of grip as the tread overheats and the surface grains or blisters. **Wear** is the physical loss of rubber. Strategy engineers model both: a tyre that grains on lap eight may still have tread left, but the working surface is ruined.

The **undercut** (covered in the strategy note) is more powerful when the incoming compound is significantly faster than the aged tyre. If compounds are close in pace, the undercut shrinks and track position plus **overcut** in clean air can win.

Pirelli publishes a recommended operating window in degrees Celsius. Teams heat blankets in the garage; once on track, drivers weave and manage brake-by-wire and ERS deployment to keep the fronts in the window without overheating the rears.

**Qualifying** usually uses the softest available slick. Parc fermé rules then lock the car; race-start tyre choice is a strategic decision (often starting on the compound used in Q2 under older rules, later relaxed).

**Sprint** weekends compress practice, which reduces tyre-learning laps. Teams sometimes guess compound offsets from previous events at similar asphalt.

Wet weather: intermediates for a drying or damp track; full wets for standing water. Crossing the crossover point too early on slicks is a classic error (see 2011 Canada and several Spa races). The halo does not change tyre physics; standing water and aquaplaning still decide whether a car can apply throttle.

Construction (sidewall stiffness, belt angles) is homologated. Teams cannot legally change compounds mid-weekend except by using the nominated set. Illegal tyre tricks (undercutting operating pressure minima) have drawn FIA scrutiny because low pressure increases contact patch and can overheat the carcass.

## Stint planning and pit-stop windows

Race engineers publish a "tyre delta" table: how many tenths a new C3 is worth versus a 15-lap C2. Combined with pit-loss (typically 20–28 seconds depending on pit lane speed limits), this table decides whether a two-stop beats a one-stop.

Safety cars bunch the field and reset tyre deltas because everyone can pit under a reduced time loss. A well-timed safety car is worth more than a perfect undercut. Virtual safety cars reduce the saving because the pit lane is not as relatively cheap.

Track evolution (rubber laid down) often makes the race faster than practice, which can flip the compound offset. Teams therefore keep one set of the medium for a flexible second stint even if the model preferred all-hard.

**Camber, toe, and pressure** targets are set per circuit. High-speed tracks like Monza need lower drag and careful rear-tyre temperatures; street circuits like Monaco live on mechanical grip and often the softest slicks.

Pirelli's mandatory-set rules (you must return certain sets) exist so qualifying does not consume the whole allocation. The allocation has changed year to year; always check the sporting regulations for the current season rather than memorizing a single number.

Inner-shoulder graining on the left-front at clockwise circuits with heavy traction zones (Barcelona, Austin) is a common complaint. Drivers are coached to rotate the car with throttle and differential maps to unload that shoulder.

None of this requires naming a bouncing aerodynamic mode; it is purely contact-patch management.
